# Culture and Civilization

**Status:** active working canon.

This document owns the transition from environment and history to institutions,
cultural constraints, and civilization regimes.

## 1. Culture is a solution set

Culture is not a costume set, species label, visual style, or isolated pantheon.
It is an accumulated set of solutions to recurring problems.

A culture can encode repeated experience through:

`names + vocabulary + food + family + property + work + time + death + law + religion + technology + knowledge + memory + risk + art`.

The goal is not to fill every slot. The goal is to make the chosen slots causally
specific.

## 2. Core distinction

`lineage → biological constraints`

`environment → pressures`

`history → path dependence`

`culture → norms and interpretation`

`institutions → durable enforcement`

`civilization → organized reproduction of the whole solution set`

A single lineage may contain many cultures, and a single civilization may contain
multiple lineages.

## 3. Civilizational equation

A civilization is derived from:

`GEOGRAPHY + RESOURCE + HISTORY + TECHNOLOGY + KNOWLEDGE + RISK + INSTITUTIONS + CULTURE`.

The reverse test is also required: if a proposed civilization cannot be
constructed from the existing world foundation without a new fundamental law,
then either the civilization proposal or the foundation needs review.

## 4. Cultural fingerprint

When a major culture is authored, start with the small number of categories that
actually create different behavior:

- food and survival;
- family and inheritance;
- property and ownership;
- work and apprenticeship;
- time horizon;
- death and memory;
- law and prohibition;
- religion/philosophy;
- technology and maintenance;
- knowledge and secrecy;
- relation to other peoples;
- risk and failure;
- language and naming.

## 5. Ordinary-life specificity

Culture becomes real when it changes mundane decisions.

Useful probes:

- What is embarrassing here?
- What is a normal debt?
- What counts as ownership?
- What is inherited: money, land, duties, knowledge, memory?
- What is unacceptable even when legal?
- What do people joke about?
- What do they fear losing?
- What is sacred because of a historical failure?
- What does a stranger misunderstand immediately?

## 6. Civilizational regimes

These are **working regime patterns**, not a final world atlas. They exist to
prove that different civilizational logics can grow from the same substrate.

| Regime | Main pressure | Main resource/advantage | Main dependency |
|---|---|---|---|
| Central riverine states | scarcity + politics | water, food, trade | transport and fertile land |
| Forest civilizations | ecological continuity | living systems, biological knowledge | ecosystem recovery |
| Mountain cities | infrastructure | minerals, engineering | maintenance and supply |
| Industrial basins | scale | energy, capital, manufacturing | supply chains |
| Maritime / pirate arc | mobility | cargo, information, routes | ports and legitimacy |
| Necropolis belt | memory | information, genealogy, Echo | preservation and trust |
| Underwater cities | habitat integrity | controlled environment, deep resources | life support |
| Sky enclaves | stability | strategic position, legacy infrastructure | extreme maintenance |
| Closed technology city | monopoly | specialist knowledge, rare components | external inputs and skilled people |
| Refuge city | social exclusion | mixed skills and information | water and political tolerance |
| Ruin frontier | uncertainty | relics and knowledge | access to ancient sites |
| Cosmic frontier | distance | energy, logistics, research | extreme infrastructure |

A regime is interesting when it has both an advantage and a dependency that can
turn into pressure.

## 7. Baseline human cultures

Humans are particularly capable of borrowing, mixing, migrating, standardizing
and changing norms quickly. This supports trade networks, industrialization and
cultural hybridization.

The cost is faster loss of traditions and weaker continuity than in cultures
built around very long memory or infrastructure maintenance.

## 8. Sylvan cultural pressures

Long life changes the value of time. Decades-long debt, apprenticeship and
ownership become culturally distinct.

Possible results include:

- living infrastructure;
- long apprenticeships;
- inheritance disputes over things planted or maintained for decades;
- memorial landscapes;
- preservationist and industrialist factions;
- high value placed on ecological continuity.

None of these creates a single "elf culture". They are pressure vectors from
biology + environment + history.

## 9. Mountain cultural pressures

Maintenance can become a moral category as well as a technical one.

Possible institutions include:

- infrastructure houses;
- professional certification;
- maintenance inheritance;
- food-storage law;
- inspection authority;
- traditions that distinguish preserve/rebuild/break attitudes toward ancient
  systems.

A tunnel can be legally owned by one family while safety responsibility belongs
to another office or lineage. That distinction can generate politics without new
magic.

## 10. Deepwater cultural pressures

Where every cubic meter and every pressure boundary matters:

- space becomes social capital;
- habitat integrity becomes law;
- privacy becomes an architectural problem;
- specialists who keep air/water systems stable can outrank warriors;
- sabotage can be treated as a threat to the whole population.

These pressures can produce strong surface-vs-independence conflicts.

## 11. Necropolis cultural pressures

The central question is:

> Who owns the memory of the dead?

A necropolis may store bodies, genealogies, testimonies, records, artifacts and
Echo traces. That makes memory a legal and political institution rather than a
generic gothic aesthetic.

Possible factions include preservationists, liberators, archivists and identity
purists. None requires a definitive soul answer.

## 12. Industrial cultural pressures

Industrial society turns repeatability, standardization and measurable output into
social forces.

The key contradiction is:

`greater production → greater dependence on infrastructure and supply chains`.

Industrial development therefore produces workers, engineers, financiers,
regulators, labor politics, environmental costs and conflicts over who controls
the bottleneck.

## 13. Maritime / pirate regime

Piracy is not a race or a nation. It is a political-economic niche:

`high cargo value + weak enforcement + geographic advantage + information asymmetry`

A pirate economy can be highly sophisticated in mobility and route knowledge while
remaining dependent on agricultural and industrial surplus produced elsewhere.

This is useful because not every compelling regime needs a special Resonance
school.

## 14. Sky enclaves

A sky city is a giant infrastructure machine, not a floating castle.

It must solve:

`stabilization + water + food + materials + energy + transport + repair`.

The core cultural split is preservation versus descent versus expansion. The
advantage is strategic geography; the weakness is legacy-system dependency.

## 15. Closed technology city

A city can become technologically dominant by controlling a rare node, material,
process or specialist class.

The recurring loop is:

`knowledge → monopoly → wealth → closure → stagnation risk → external dependence`.

This creates strong politics without needing an evil technocracy by default.

## 16. Refuge city

The source corpus proposes a city whose systemic role is social rather than purely
environmental: a place for people, skills and knowledge that other societies have
trouble absorbing.

It can connect migration, medicine, rehabilitation, black markets, neutral trade,
information and social mobility. The important test is not "is it a cool city?"
but "what wider system becomes possible because it exists?"

## 17. Ruin frontier

An archaeological frontier can connect:

`history ↔ economy ↔ knowledge`.

Its central conflict is whether ruins should be preserved, studied, inhabited,
restored, dismantled or sold.

For locals, ruins may be ordinary infrastructure or home rather than heritage
sites. That asymmetry is a useful source of conflict.

## 18. Cultural prohibitions

The strongest culture design pattern in the source corpus is a prohibition that
arose from historical memory or material pressure.

Use:

`historical event → repeated cost → institutional lesson → prohibition → ordinary behavior`.

A prohibition should explain what people refuse to do even when doing it would be
technically possible.

## 19. Back-propagation rule

Civilization hypotheses are allowed to force revision of upstream world
foundations.

Example:

`new civilization → impossible without hydrology history → revise Great Fracture model`.

That is not a contradiction of the earlier work. It is a healthy back-propagation
loop. However, it should happen only when a concrete civilization requires it.

## 20. Authoring rule

A new civilization should answer:

`What pressure dominates here? What advantage does it exploit? What dependency does it create? What institution manages that dependency? What culture makes that institution feel normal? What historical residue explains why it is this way?`

If the answer is mostly aesthetic, the civilization is not ready.
